Rosey: privacy policy

Last updated: October 16, 2026

Rosey is a personal assistant app built by Daniel Davis for his own use. It is not offered to the public. This page explains what the app does with data.

Microphone

While "Hey Rosey" is turned on, the app listens on the phone for its wake phrase. That listening happens entirely on the device using an on-device speech model. Audio is not recorded, stored or sent anywhere until the wake phrase is heard or the user opens a conversation.

To make sure only the owner's voice wakes it, the app keeps a voiceprint on the phone: a short list of numbers worked out from six recordings of the owner saying "Hey Rosey" in the app's settings. The recordings themselves are not kept. The voiceprint never leaves the phone, is not included in backups, and is deleted when the app is uninstalled. While the phone's screen is covered (in a pocket, for example), the app does not listen at all; this pocket guard stays on unless the user switches it off in the app's settings.

During a conversation, the user's voice is streamed to ElevenLabs, the voice service that powers the conversation, and the text of the conversation is saved to the owner's private server.

Personal information

When the user asks about their own information (for example reminders, to-dos, calendar, messages or email), the request is sent to the owner's private server, which is reachable only over the owner's private network. Answers that include personal information require the phone to be unlocked.

On the server, the assistant's thinking is done by Anthropic's Claude AI service: the text it works on (questions, typed messages, forwarded notifications, and contact details it looks up) is sent to Anthropic for that purpose, under Anthropic's commercial terms.

Notifications

The app shows notifications about items that need the user. Notification pings delivered through Google's push service carry no message content; the app fetches the details from the owner's private server.

Notification access (optional)

If the user turns it on (the app explains it first, then Android asks), the app reads the notifications that arrive on the phone: text messages, missed calls, and alerts from other apps such as a bank or a delivery service. It sends the app's name, the sender and the notification's text to the owner's private server, where the assistant (using Anthropic's Claude, see above) decides what needs the user's attention. Before anything leaves the phone, the app removes the one-time codes and sign-in links it can recognise; the server applies the same check again. Notifications from password managers and authenticator apps are never sent. The user can turn this off, or switch off single apps, in the app's settings. The app never taps a notification's buttons, replies or sends a message on its own. When the user asks the assistant to answer a text, the assistant only prepares a draft. The app shows the exact words and who they go to. Only when the user then says so out loud during a live conversation in the app, or taps Send twice, does the app hand that reply to the Reply button of that conversation's own notification, and the user's messaging app sends it. For a new text, the app opens the user's messaging app with the person and the words filled in, and the user sends it there. The app does not use SMS permissions. Drafts and what happened to them (sent, opened, failed) are kept on the owner's private server.

Contacts (optional)

If the user allows it, the app sends the names, phone numbers, email addresses and company names of the phone's contacts to the owner's private server about twice a day, so the assistant can tell who texted, called or emailed. Photos, notes and addresses are not sent.

Typed messages

Messages the user types to the assistant in the app are sent to the owner's private server, answered there, and kept with the conversation history.

Places and drive times (Google Maps)

When the user asks Rosey about a place (opening hours, a drive time, or a store that isn't on the app's store map), the owner's server sends Google Maps Platform the words of that search and the point it is measured from: the phone's last reported location, the owner's home or shop, or a town. Nothing else from the conversation, and no emails, texts or contacts, goes to Google. These lookups only run while the user is talking or typing to Rosey, never while she reads messages. Google's use of this data is covered by Google's privacy policy and the Google Maps terms.

Phone actions in Rosey Live

When you ask Rosey Live for a timer, an alarm, directions, music, the flashlight, an app, a phone call or a WhatsApp message, the Rosey app does it on your phone, only after you asked for it in your own words. As with everything you say in Rosey Live, your words go to ElevenLabs' voice service. For a call or a WhatsApp message, the app looks the person up in your phone's own contacts on the phone and shows you a card with the name and number. Only the person's name (or, when two contacts share a name, their names) goes to the voice service, never their numbers. Nothing is dialed or opened until you confirm by voice or by tapping. If you allow Rosey to make phone calls (an Android permission you can take back at any time), she places the call once you confirm; otherwise your phone's dialer opens and you tap Call yourself. WhatsApp opens with your message filled in, and you tap Send there. To give directions to home or the shop, the app asks your own server for those saved places. To control music, the app uses the notification access you already gave it to see what is playing; when you ask what's playing, the song's title and artist go to the voice service. Rosey never changes Do Not Disturb or your volume.

What is not done

  • No data is sold or shared with advertisers or data brokers.
  • No advertising and no analytics or tracking SDKs.
  • Data is sent only to the owner's private server, to Anthropic for the assistant's thinking, to ElevenLabs during spoken conversations, and to Google Maps Platform for place and drive-time lookups, always encrypted in transit (TLS).

Keeping and deleting data

Conversation transcripts are kept on the owner's private server and can be deleted by the owner at any time. Forwarded notifications are kept in their own list on the server for 30 days; what the assistant read also stays in its conversation history (and that history's backups) until the owner deletes it. Uninstalling the app removes everything stored on the phone.
